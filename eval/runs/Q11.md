## Literature review

Evaluating the factual accuracy of large language models involves diverse frameworks, benchmark datasets, and performance trends, while also encountering notable limitations.

To address evaluation methodologies, OpenFactCheck functions as a unified framework featuring RESPONSEEVAL, LLMEVAL, and CHECKEREVAL modules [1]. Additionally, the FEWL metric's effectiveness in measuring and mitigating hallucinations is demonstrated through experiments using benchmarks such as Truthful-QA, CHALE, and HaluEval [3]. Other benchmark datasets utilized to measure these issues include DefAn, which consists of over 20,000 unique prompts spanning eight domains [2]. 

Regarding performance results and trends, specialized frameworks have shown significant improvements in factual reliability. For instance, the ConfLVLM framework successfully reduces the error rate of generated claims for scene descriptions from LLaVa-1.5 from 87.8% down to 10.0%, achieving a 95.3% true positive rate in filtering erroneous claims [4]. Furthermore, RULE demonstrates an average improvement of 47.4% in factual accuracy across three datasets concerning medical visual question answering (VQA) and report generation tasks [5].

Despite these advancements, several challenges persist in assessing factual accuracy. Large language models remain vulnerable to hallucinations and response deviation, creating concerns about their trustworthiness and reliability in academic and research settings [7]. Furthermore, current factuality evaluation resources face significant limitations: they are frequently constrained to specific tasks or domains, restricted to simpler verification tasks like claim verification, or are simply too small to facilitate the training of new evaluators [6].
